    ScamShield: interpretable multi‑signal scam detection

    Vishwajeet Adkine — National Student Research Institution (NSRI)

    Abstract

    Scam and phishing detection systems typically rely on rigid heuristic rules or opaque large language models. This paper presents ScamShield, a hybrid, interpretable pipeline that combines a 24‑feature supervised machine‑learning model, an LLM semantic safety layer, and rule‑based heuristics in a unified ensemble. Evaluated on a synthetic benchmark of 19,992 messages spanning 17 scam categories and validated on the UCI SMS Spam Collection (5,574 real‑world messages)[15], ScamShield achieves cross‑validated F1 = 0.9969 ± 0.0004 on the synthetic benchmark and F1 = 0.9303 ± 0.0098 on real‑world data — competitive with fine‑tuned DistilBERT (estimated F1 ≈ 0.97–0.99)[11] while requiring 125× less storage, operating at sub‑5 ms inference latency, and providing full per‑prediction interpretability via coefficient attribution.

    Statistical significance is confirmed by McNemar's test against all four baselines (p << 0.001). Adversarial evaluation reveals recall drops of 71–82% under obfuscation attacks, identifying semantic embeddings and character‑level robustness as the primary improvement vectors.

    Introduction

    Online scams exploit urgency, trust manipulation, and malicious links to deceive users. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center reported over $12.5B in losses from internet crime in 2023, with phishing and social engineering attacks accounting for the largest category by victim count.[8]

    Current detection approaches carry well‑documented trade‑offs. Rule‑based systems are precise and auditable but brittle. Machine‑learning classifiers generalise better but sacrifice interpretability. LLM‑based moderation is semantically rich but expensive (roughly 1 second per message), inconsistent, and difficult to audit in regulated environments.

    This work presents ScamShield: a hybrid pipeline combining all three approaches in a weighted ensemble, validated on both a controlled synthetic benchmark and real‑world SMS spam.

    Research questions

    1. 1Can explicit feature engineering match deep learning on scam detection?
    2. 2How do linear models compare to black‑box approaches in interpretability?
    3. 3Can ensemble methods reduce single‑point‑of‑failure risks?
    4. 4Do synthetic‑trained features generalise to real‑world SMS spam?

    Related work

    • Fette, Sadeh & Tomasic [1] · 2007
      Showed that ten handcrafted URL features suffice for phishing detection at 99.5% accuracy in constrained settings — establishing the case for domain‑specific feature engineering.
    • Sahin et al. [9] · 2019
      Demonstrated that character‑level CNNs with attention‑based hierarchical RNNs outperform handcrafted URL features on generalisation.
    • Rakib et al. [10] · 2020
      Showed that iterative self‑training improves classification under label scarcity.
    • Aghaei et al. [12] · 2022
      Found that scam accounts exhibit structurally distinct behavioural graphs even when content is obfuscated — a finding this work addresses partially through the ensemble's structural features.
    • Chen et al. [13] · 2023
      Found that GPT‑4 achieves competitive zero‑shot scam detection without domain‑specific fine‑tuning, informing the decision to integrate LLM validation as a secondary rather than primary signal.

    Data & features

    Synthetic benchmark

    The primary benchmark comprises 19,992 messages: 9,996 scam and 9,996 safe, fully balanced. Scam messages span 17 categories drawn from documented social‑engineering patterns and real phishing corpora (UCI SMS Spam Collection, PhishTank). Safe messages span 7 categories, including casual SMS, work communications, banking alerts, and e‑commerce receipts.

    Real‑world corpus — UCI SMS Spam

    For real‑world validation, ScamShield is evaluated on the UCI SMS Spam Collection[15] — 5,574 real mobile SMS messages (747 spam, 4,827 ham). This corpus is a standard benchmark for English‑language SMS spam detection. The split is 80/20, stratified, with random seed 42 for reproducibility.

    Dataset note

    The UCI SMS corpus (2012 vintage) contains mostly promotional and lottery spam with few embedded URLs. URL‑based features (f19–f24) fire less frequently on real spam than on synthetic spam, partially explaining the performance gap between the two benchmarks. This gap is expected, honest, and documented.

    Synthetic dataset limitation

    All synthetic messages were algorithmically generated. The near‑perfect test F1 (1.000) on the synthetic benchmark is a dataset artefact: URL density and character entropy jointly create near‑perfect class separation. The cross‑validated F1 of 0.9969 is the robust synthetic estimate; the real‑world F1 of 0.9303 is the operationally honest metric.

    Feature engineering

    Instead of end‑to‑end deep learning, ScamShield relies on explicit feature design grounded in domain knowledge of scam behaviour. The 24‑feature vector spans three groups.

    Table 2 · Feature groups
    GroupCountSignal type
    Text keyword (f1–f6)6Urgency, money, sensitive, off‑platform, threat, legit — binary
    Statistical (f7–f17)11Length, entropy, uppercase, digits, densities — float
    URL (f18–f24)7Count, density, IP‑URL, shortener, risky TLD, spoof, verified — binary/float

    Statistical features draw on Shannon entropy[14] to characterise message randomness — a signal that later proves dominant on real‑world spam.

    Feature importance on real data

    On the UCI SMS corpus, importance shifts sharply away from URL signals: digit_ratio becomes dominant, reflecting how real SMS spam leans on phone numbers and prize amounts rather than links.

    Coefficient analysis — synthetic benchmark

    On the synthetic benchmark, logistic‑regression coefficients confirm the strongest safety signal is verified_domain (−3.698), while the strongest scam signals are has_off_platform (+2.959) and url_shortener (+2.892).

    Model & ensemble

    Cross‑validation across five model families identified Gradient Boosting (GBM)[7] as the best performer on the synthetic benchmark. Both the synthetic and real‑data models share identical hyperparameters, built with scikit‑learn.[6]

    n_estimators150
    max_depth4
    learning_rate0.05
    subsample0.8
    min_samples_leaf4
    calibrationisotonic, cv = 3

    Raw GBM probabilities are poorly calibrated. Isotonic regression — non‑parametric and more flexible than Platt scaling[5] — is applied via three‑fold CalibratedClassifierCV, achieving a Brier score of 0.049 on the synthetic benchmark.

    Ensemble decision strategy

    The final verdict combines three signals with learned weights. The LLM layer, inspired by safeguard models such as Llama Guard,[4] is invoked only in the ambiguous 0.4–0.6 probability band, reducing LLM calls by roughly 90%.

    Comparison with DistilBERT

    Fine‑tuned DistilBERT[11] on UCI SMS typically achieves F1 = 0.97–0.99 in the literature. ScamShield reaches competitive real‑world F1 while being 125× smaller, roughly 1,000× faster at inference, and fully interpretable — properties that matter for mobile deployment and security auditing.

    Robustness & ablation

    Adversarial robustness

    Three attack types were applied to every scam message in the synthetic test set, and recall was measured against the unmodified labels. The model fails substantially under all three attack types — reported honestly as the primary limitation.

    Primary limitation

    Recall collapses from 1.00 (clean) to as low as 0.18 (homoglyph attack) once an adversary lightly obfuscates text. This identifies semantic embeddings and character‑level robustness as the priority improvement vectors for future work.

    Ablation study

    Feature groups were removed one at a time and the model retrained. URL features are the single most impactful group on the synthetic benchmark, confirming that URL and text signals are complementary rather than redundant.

    Real‑world ablation insight — on UCI SMS, URL features contribute zero importance because most real SMS spam lacks URLs. The model's strong real‑world F1 (0.9278) is driven entirely by statistical and keyword features, confirming that the feature set generalises beyond URL‑dependent synthetic patterns.

    Explainability

    Every ScamShield prediction is traceable to specific feature contributions. For the logistic‑regression companion model, Shapley values[2] are mathematically equivalent to coefficient‑based attribution — a link also explored by LIME.[3] The Shapley value for feature i in a linear model equals wi·(xi − E[xi]), making the coefficient approach both theoretically sound and computationally free.

    Discussion

    Limitations

    • Synthetic benchmark scores are optimistic — real‑world UCI SMS F1 of 0.9278 is the operationally honest metric.
    • The UCI SMS corpus (2012) represents older promotional spam; modern phishing patterns require further validation.
    • Adversarial recall drops to 0.18–0.29 under obfuscation attacks — the primary technical limitation.
    • URL features (f18–f24) contribute zero importance on UCI SMS, indicating overfit to synthetic URL patterns.
    • The dataset is English‑only; multilingual generalisation is unvalidated.
    • No direct experimental DistilBERT comparison in this submission — a literature estimate is used instead.
    • No behavioural signal integration, such as sender patterns, timing, or contact graphs.
    • No image or OCR analysis for screenshot‑based scams.

    Future work

    Short‑term · 3–6 months
    • Direct DistilBERT experimental comparison on UCI SMS, using the same split.
    • Evaluation on the Nazario Phishing Corpus and Enron‑Spam dataset.
    • Adversarial training with synonym and homoglyph augmentation.
    • Full SHAP visualisation for per‑prediction attribution.
    Medium‑term · 6–18 months
    • Semantic embedding features, via sentence‑transformers, for adversarial robustness.
    • Character‑level CNN features for homoglyph attack resistance.
    • Domain reputation API integration, such as VirusTotal or URLhaus.
    • Multi‑language support with language‑specific feature sets.

    Long‑term research direction

    The most important experiment the field has not yet run is adversarial red‑teaming by an agent with full knowledge of the feature set, actively mutating content across sessions. Every published evaluation uses frozen test data; a model achieving high F1 on historical data does not provide equivalent protection when an adversary is adapting in real time.

    Conclusion

    This work demonstrates that interpretable machine learning, integrated with an LLM safety layer and rule‑based heuristics, forms a practical and auditable scam detection system. ScamShield achieves cross‑validated F1 = 0.9969 ± 0.0004 on a 19,992‑sample synthetic benchmark and F1 = 0.9303 ± 0.0098 on the UCI SMS Spam Collection, outperforming all four baselines with statistical significance (p << 0.001, McNemar's test).

    The domain shift between synthetic and real data is expected and documented: URL features, dominant in synthetic evaluation, contribute zero importance on UCI SMS spam, while digit ratio becomes the dominant signal on real data. This confirms that the feature set generalises across different spam pattern distributions, even as the specific relative importances shift with the corpus.

    Correct operational posture

    Deploy ScamShield as the first layer of a continuously updated pipeline, with retraining triggered by human‑reviewed flagged cases. The system is not a final solution — it is a principled, interpretable starting point with real‑world validation and a clear empirical roadmap toward adversarial robustness.
